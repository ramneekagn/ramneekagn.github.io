A review of Scaling sparse feature circuit finding for in-context learning.

Created on September 14, 2026

2026   ·   LLMs   In Context Learning   Mechanistic Interpretability   ·   research

Context

I wrote this review for the seminar “Building Trust in Large Language Models” at Ruhr University Bochum for the paper “Scaling sparse feature circuit finding for in-context learning” by Kharlapenko et al a few weeks ago.

Key Takeaways

In this paper, Kharlapenko et al posit four goals:

  1. Demonstrate the usage of sparse feature circuits (SFC) on a greater scale.

  2. Gain a deeper level of understanding of in-context learning (ICL).

  3. Demonstrate effectiveness of sparse autoencoders (SAEs) through deconstructing the mechanisms of ICL .

  4. Pave the way for monitoring and controlling ICL to improve safety/capability.

Models have been demonstrated to improve their performance with ICL, where the context in the input of the model guides the output of the model. As the underlying mechanism as to why ICL occurs is unknown, the paper attempts to address this via techniques of mechanistic interpretability, while simultaneously exploring the usefulness of mechanistic interpretability methods. ICL tasks can be things such as performing antonyms or language translation, the examples for which are given in a specific format ($X_1 \rightarrow Y_1, X_2 \rightarrow Y_2, \dots, X_N \rightarrow Y_N$), where $X_i$ is the input and $Y_i$ the expected output.

This work builds upon the concept of the task vector. A task vector is formed by passing multiple prompts of the same ICL task through the model and averaging the individual activations of a specific token at an internal residual stream layer. These task vectors can be seen as a compressed vector representation of a specific ICL task. If a task vector is added to the residual stream during inference, prior research has demonstrated that the task can be performed zero-shot with higher accuracy. However, task vectors are inherently abstract, out-of-distribution vectors that are useful for steering but not for detailed interpretation. Furthermore, a limitation is their effectiveness for steering, since they are averages of multiple different activations, and steering with such vectors often affects other capabilities of the model.

The researchers attempt to interpret task vectors further with SAEs, which are an architecture that transforms activation vectors into a sparse, higher dimensional space using an encoder, and then reconstructs them back into the original shape using a decoder. This sparse latent space allows, in the optimal case, one latent in the SAE to be attributed to one feature. A feature being a (human interpretable) concept that is represented in the language model. The core motivation behind this is that this 1:1 alignment allows for more precise steering/suppression, and easier interpretation of the activation vector. However, when applied on task vectors, this method fails. The task loss in the reconstruction is much higher compared to the original task vector, which means that the decomposition with the encoder is likely inaccurate, furthermore, it results in 10 to 20 latents of the SAE activating instead of the optimal 1.

To solve this problem, they devised the Task Vector Cleaning algorithm. At its core, it is an algorithm that optimizes the SAE latent vector $\theta$ for every task vector. For each task vector, the method maintains an optimized latent vector $\theta$. As a result, the reconstruction generated by $\theta$ achieves a task loss comparable to the original task vector, indicating a significantly better reconstruction, while activating only 2 to 4 latents instead of 10 to 20.

Concretely, Figure 1{reference-type=”ref” reference=”fig:tvc_visual”} shows the mechanics of the algorithm. The authors take a concrete task vector, feed it into the residual stream of the LLM (in Figure 1{reference-type=”ref” reference=”fig:tvc_visual”}, this is after block 1 for simplicity). The task vector gets passed through the SAE, de- and reconstructed, and finally injected into the residual stream after the norm and add operation. For a 0-shot prompt for the same task, target tokens are predicted with the LLM. Optimally, the LLM should act exactly as if the original task vector is injected and have a high performance, therefore a low negative log likelihood to the target token. Additionally, in the loss, the L1 norm of the activation vector is considered to keep it sparse. The authors update the sparse activation vector with the gradient of the loss to obtain an improved sparse activation vector for the task vector.

In their visualizations (Figure 6, 10, 11 Kharlapenko et al), they only kept the latent that showed the highest effect on the ICL tasks. These latents are called task execution features. An interesting aspect of these is that they mostly activate at the → token, which is right before the output token (Example: Hot → Cold, Wet → Dry, Sunny →). The authors make the claim that these execution features are directly responsible for executing the ICL task. They aim to prove this with a steering experiment. They increase the activations of the individual task execution features, and measure the effect on different ICL tasks. What they find is that one task execution vector oftentimes affects one ICL task.

They build upon prior work by scaling the SFC methodology proposed by Marks et al to larger models, such as Gemma-1 2B. Fundamentally, the methodology builds on SAEs by placing multiple SAEs across different Transformer blocks. Then, the indirect effect on the output is measured, which quantifies, via a metric $m$, how much the output $x$ is affected by intervening on a specific SAE latent $a$ in the computational graph: \(\begin{equation} IE(m; a) = m(x | \text{do}(a = a')) - m(x) \end{equation}\) Only the most causally relevant SAE latents are retained. The authors made minor adjustments to adapt the SFC method for larger models, including modifying the loss function and aggregating features by token types.

Using the SFC methodology, the authors were able to identify task detection features in a previous Transformer block (Transformer block 11: task-detection, block 12: task-execution features). Concretely, the task-detection features recognize which task is being demonstrated in prompt and assist in activating the correct executor. They activate the most at the output tokens (Example: Hot -> Cold, Dry -> Wet; they activate heavily at Cold and Wet). They are demonstrated to be largely causally connected to the task execution features of the same task. However, Figure 11 (Kharlapenko et al.) shows that model executors and detectors do not just correlate to their own task, but other task detectors can have a causal effect on other executors. For example, individual translation detectors strongly affect all executors that translate between different languages. Finally, the authors highlighted that not all detectors have a strong effect on the executors.

Strengths

The paper effectively builds upon existing literature by combining different mechanistic interpretability methods to analyze ICL. The authors succeed in achieving one of their primary goals, which is scaling SFC to larger models than previously studied while requiring only minor adjustments to adapt the methodology. Additionally, SAEs proved effective at decomposing task vectors when paired with the novel Task Vector Cleaning (TVC) algorithm. The authors further demonstrated the robustness of the TVC algorithm by replicating their experiments across different models and model sizes.

The TVC algorithm represents a key strength of this work, as it enables the accurate deconstruction of task vectors using SAEs into the features. Another strength lies in the experimental rigor, as the authors validate the causal roles of task-execution and task-detection features through steering experiments and observe changes in model outputs across individual ICL tasks.

The application of SAEs to ICL tasks offers valuable opportunities for internal model auditing, as high-level behaviors can be clearly mapped to specific features. Features associated with undesirable behaviors or safety risks introduced via ICL could potentially be monitored or steered in future applications. In particular, distinguishing between task-detection and task-execution features is highly useful because one could monitor task-detection features to identify malicious intent within a prompt while actively suppressing the corresponding task-execution features. Taking this a step further, a promising direction would be fine-tuning the model to unlearn these harmful execution features, as demonstrated by Ashuach et al.

In conclusion, the paper advances a highly relevant area of research, particularly given that jailbreaking LLMs often relies on manipulating the context window and abusing ICL capabilities (Wei et al).

Weaknesses

General

A notable limitation lies in the gap between the paper’s overarching objectives and its concrete findings. While the study provides valuable structural insights into feature circuits, it leaves open questions regarding how ICL fundamentally functions within LLMs to drive improvements on specific tasks. Furthermore, while the authors highlight the potential of their methodology for improving model safety and capability control, the study focuses primarily on feature discovery rather than demonstrating a practical application of its findings.

Additionally, the proposed methodology was only evaluated on small-to-medium models (2B and 9B parameters). Consequently, it remains uncertain whether the TVC algorithm and circuit extraction techniques scale effectively to larger, frontier-scale models. The evaluation is also restricted to a narrow set of tasks, primarily simple linguistic transformations, basic coding, and general knowledge retrieval, which leaves capabilities such as mathematical reasoning unexplored. Moreover, the ICL tasks were presented in a rigid, synthetic format ($X \rightarrow Y$), which may not reflect how ICL functions in complex, real-world prompts.

As noted in the paper’s limitations, multiple competing execution and detection features often fire for the same task, and their alignment is not always consistent (as shown in Figure 11 (Kharlapenko et al)). Lastly, mechanistic interpretability approaches inherently require full access to internal model weights and residual stream activations, meaning these techniques cannot be applied to audit or interpret closed-source API models.

Sparse Autoencoders

The methodology relies heavily on SAEs, whose scaling remains computationally and methodologically challenging. Although Gao et al successfully trained an SAE on GPT-4, doing so remains computationally expensive, especially when combined with SFC, which require training and running multiple SAEs across several model layers. Furthermore, SAEs must be trained individually for every specific model and layer, rendering them inflexible across different architectures.

Another critique of SAEs concerns feature atomicity within their latent space. Leask et al demonstrated that the granularity and composition of latent features vary depending on the SAE width, suggesting that SAE latents may only capture an incomplete subset of true underlying features. They further hypothesize that there may be no single SAE width capable of capturing the true atomic feature space of an MLP.

Therefore, auditing task-detection and task-execution features via SAEs may introduce errors if the learned latent space does not represent true atomic features. For instance, if a task-execution feature actually represents a composite superset of multiple sub-features, ablating it might impair unintended model capabilities. Finally, any feature not captured by the SAE will remain invisible to monitoring, auditing, and steering interventions.
